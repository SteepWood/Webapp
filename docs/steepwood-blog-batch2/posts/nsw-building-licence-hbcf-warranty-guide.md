A NSW building licence check for a renovation takes minutes and can prevent a serious mistake. For residential building work above [$5,000 including GST](https://www.nsw.gov.au/business-and-economy/running-a-business/advertising-laws-and-your-business/building-trade-advertisements), NSW requires the relevant contractor licence; eligible work at [$20,000 including GST or more](https://www.hiainsurance.com.au/products/home-warranty-insurance/home-warranty-insurance-nsw) brings Home Building Compensation Fund requirements. Check the licence, written contract and insurance certificate before any deposit. This article is general information, not legal advice; obtain legal advice for your contract or dispute.

Joinery is easy to underestimate because it arrives late in a renovation. By the time cabinetry is measured, a homeowner may already be committed to a builder, a design and a move-in date. The legal checks should happen earlier. They are not an accusation against a contractor. A legitimate operator should expect them.

## The short answer

Before paying a deposit, identify the legal contracting party, use the [NSW Government licence-check service](https://www.service.nsw.gov.au/transaction/check-a-builder-or-tradesperson-licence), compare the result with the proposed work, read the written scope and payment schedule, and ask whether HBCF applies. Keep copies of every document. This sequence is general information, not legal advice.

## The three thresholds that decide everything: $5,000, $20,000 and the contract type

The first threshold is [$5,000 including GST](https://www.nsw.gov.au/business-and-economy/running-a-business/advertising-laws-and-your-business/building-trade-advertisements). NSW states that a contractor licence is required to carry out, advertise or contract for residential building work above that amount in the circumstances described by its licensing rules. Kitchen, bathroom and laundry renovation categories are specifically addressed in the NSW categories-of-work guidance ([NSW Government](https://www.nsw.gov.au/business-and-economy/licences-and-credentials/building-and-trade-licences-and-registrations/categories-of-work)).

The second threshold is [$20,000 including GST](https://www.hiainsurance.com.au/products/home-warranty-insurance/home-warranty-insurance-nsw). HIA Insurance Services explains that HBCF insurance is required for eligible residential building work at or above that value and that the certificate must be given before the builder takes a deposit or other payment. Eligibility and exclusions need to be checked against the actual contract; do not treat a blog as a coverage determination.

The contract type matters too. HIA Insurance Services describes written-contract requirements above [$5,000](https://www.hiainsurance.com.au/products/home-warranty-insurance/home-warranty-insurance-nsw) and additional large-job contract requirements above [$20,000](https://www.hiainsurance.com.au/products/home-warranty-insurance/home-warranty-insurance-nsw). Ask the contractor to explain which form is being used and why. If the documents do not match the scope or value, pause and get advice before signing.

## How to check a NSW licence in five minutes

Start with the contractor's legal name, business name and licence number. Use the [Service NSW licence-check service](https://www.service.nsw.gov.au/transaction/check-a-builder-or-tradesperson-licence), not a screenshot sent by the contractor. Search the name and number, then save the result with the quote.

Next, compare what you found with the documents in front of you. Is the named licensee the person or company that will contract? Does the result show a current licence? Is the work category appropriate to the work proposed? Does the business phone number and address line up? If a company is contracting, do not assume an employee's personal credential answers the company question. Ask the contractor to explain the contracting structure in writing.

Finally, compare the scope. A small cabinetry delivery is not necessarily the same legal arrangement as a residential renovation involving removal, building work, services and installation. The correct question is not “does this person have a licence?” but “is the contracting party properly licensed for this defined work?” That distinction is worth taking to independent legal or regulatory advice if unclear.

## What a licence category actually permits

A licence is not a blanket statement that a business can do every trade. NSW issues licences and certificates for defined categories of work and provides category guidance for residential building work ([NSW Government categories of work](https://www.nsw.gov.au/business-and-economy/licences-and-credentials/building-and-trade-licences-and-registrations/categories-of-work)). The correct category depends on what is actually contracted, including whether other building trades are involved.

For cabinetry, the practical lesson is to describe the job fully before seeking a licence answer. “Kitchen” may involve cabinets, demolition, plumbing, electrical work, waterproofing, benchtops and building changes. A joiner should not represent that a carpentry credential authorises every specialist element. Ask who will do each component, whether they are separately licensed where required, and who coordinates the contract.

## What HBCF cover is, and what it is not

HBCF is not a general workmanship warranty and it is not a substitute for checking a contractor before you hire them. HIA Insurance Services explains that the scheme can respond in specified circumstances including builder insolvency, death, disappearance or licence suspension, subject to policy terms and the legislation ([HIA Insurance Services](https://www.hiainsurance.com.au/products/home-warranty-insurance/home-warranty-insurance-nsw)). It is a consumer protection mechanism for eligible home building work, not an assurance that every defect claim will be paid.

HIA Insurance Services states that the cover limit is [up to $340,000](https://www.hiainsurance.com.au/products/home-warranty-insurance/home-warranty-insurance-nsw). That is not a budget target and should never be used to select a contractor. Read the certificate, policy material and exclusions, and seek advice where the project has an unusual structure, owner-builder element or disputed scope.

## The certificate before the deposit rule

For eligible residential work at or above the threshold, the HBCF certificate must be given before the builder takes a deposit or other payment, according to [HIA Insurance Services](https://www.hiainsurance.com.au/products/home-warranty-insurance/home-warranty-insurance-nsw). Do not accept “we will send it after the deposit” as the same thing. Ask for the certificate, check the address, legal owner, contractor and contract amount, and retain it with the signed agreement.

The safest sequence is: confirm scope; confirm contracting party and licence; receive the written contract; receive and check the applicable certificate; then follow the agreed payment process. This is general information, not legal advice. If the parties, site address or price change, ask how the change affects the documents before paying a variation.

## Statutory warranties, and what counts as a major defect

NSW statutory warranties are commonly summarised as [six years for major defects and two years for other defects](https://hia.com.au/resources-and-advice/managing-your-business/managing-compliance/articles/statutory-warranties-for-home-building-work). Whether a particular issue is a major defect is a legal and factual question, not a label that a homeowner or contractor can settle by email. Keep photographs, dates, contracts, variations and communications, then obtain appropriate advice if a defect arises.

A contractor's written warranty can add useful practical commitments, such as response steps or a structural term, but it cannot remove statutory rights that apply. Read both. If a warranty document is vague about what it covers, ask for clarification before you sign rather than relying on a verbal assurance.

## What must appear in a compliant advertisement

NSW says building-trade advertisements must include the licensee name, the licence number with the correct category of work and a business telephone number in the circumstances the rules apply ([NSW Government](https://www.nsw.gov.au/business-and-economy/running-a-business/advertising-laws-and-your-business/building-trade-advertisements)). An Instagram account, a website or a vehicle sign is not a substitute for independent verification.

NSW also states that the maximum penalties for non-compliant advertising can be [$22,000 for an individual and $110,000 for a corporation](https://www.nsw.gov.au/business-and-economy/running-a-business/advertising-laws-and-your-business/building-trade-advertisements). Consumers do not need to become investigators. They need to see the disclosure, search it and stop if names or categories do not match.

## Public liability, workers compensation and why you should ask

Ask for current evidence of public liability and, where workers are engaged, workers compensation relevant to the project. The appropriate cover, policy limits and certificate requirements depend on the contractor and work. That is why a certificate should be read, not merely promised. Insurance is not a substitute for a licence, written scope or HBCF where HBCF applies.

The practical reason is simple: installation happens in a lived-in house. Floors, stairs, existing finishes, other trades and people create risk. A legitimate contractor should be able to explain what evidence it can provide and what its contract says about site protection, damage and access.

## Silica and engineered stone obligations

A responsible joinery brief names its benchtop material and fabricator. The national prohibition on engineered stone took effect on [1 July 2024](https://www.safeworkaustralia.gov.au/safety-topic/hazards/silica/engineered-stone-ban), with NSW regulatory information available from [SafeWork NSW](https://www.safework.nsw.gov.au/legal-obligations/legislation/accordians/work-health-and-safety-amendment-engineered-stone-regulation-2024). Do not accept an old specification that simply says “engineered stone”.

Sintered stone and porcelain sit outside the prohibition's engineered-stone definition in the circumstances described by [SafeWork NSW](https://www.safework.nsw.gov.au/legal-obligations/legislation/accordians/work-health-and-safety-amendment-engineered-stone-regulation-2024), but material selection still needs proper fabrication and site controls. Ask who is supplying the top, who templates it, and how it connects to the joinery contract.

## Common verification mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is checking a business name once and then assuming every later document is connected to it. The quote, contract, invoice, bank account request, licence search and insurance certificate should identify compatible parties. A small spelling difference can be innocent, but it should be explained before money moves. Save a PDF or screenshot of the official search at the time you make the decision.

The second mistake is treating a licence number as a quality rating. A licence check helps establish who is licensed and whether the details are current; it does not replace a clear scope, past work assessment, independent advice or a considered contract. Use it as one layer of due diligence. The [NSW Government advertising guidance](https://www.nsw.gov.au/business-and-economy/running-a-business/advertising-laws-and-your-business/building-trade-advertisements) requires key disclosure in the relevant advertising circumstances precisely so consumers can identify and check a contractor.

The third mistake is signing drawings that have not been read. On a joinery project, drawings should show the layout, heights, appliance and service interfaces, door swings, handles or handleless detail, internal configuration and visible end panels. A photo is not enough. If an existing wall, stone top, appliance or builder's work remains uncertain, record the allowance and decision point. This is contract discipline, not a legal conclusion.

The fourth mistake is assuming “insured” has one fixed meaning. Ask what policy is being relied upon, whether it relates to the entity that is contracting and what document can be provided. HBCF is a particular statutory scheme for eligible residential work; it does not replace public liability, workers compensation or a written warranty. The HBCF certificate timing described by [HIA Insurance Services](https://www.hiainsurance.com.au/products/home-warranty-insurance/home-warranty-insurance-nsw) is one check among several.

## A simple document sequence for a kitchen or joinery renovation

Use this sequence as a practical record-keeping method, not legal advice. First, write the desired work in a brief and confirm who is contracting. Second, use the [Service NSW licence check](https://www.service.nsw.gov.au/transaction/check-a-builder-or-tradesperson-licence) and retain the result. Third, compare the quote, drawings, materials, exclusions and GST-inclusive total. Fourth, establish whether HBCF is required for the eligible contract and receive the certificate before payment where the rule applies ([HIA Insurance Services](https://www.hiainsurance.com.au/products/home-warranty-insurance/home-warranty-insurance-nsw)). Fifth, sign only the version you have read, and approve later changes in writing.

This order gives a homeowner a clean file if the project runs well and a better record if it does not. It also gives the contractor an opportunity to correct an error before commencement. A clear paper trail is usually more valuable than a rushed verbal assurance.

If the work changes after signing, do not let the project run on a conversation alone. The changed scope, cost, tax treatment, programme effect and acceptance should be recorded together. If the change is substantial or affects eligibility, ask the contractor and an appropriate adviser how the contract and any insurance documentation should be handled. General information cannot determine that answer for an individual project.

Where a dispute has already started, obtain independent legal advice promptly. Keep the site safe, document the condition and follow the process appropriate to the contract. This guide cannot advise on liability, claim prospects, limitation periods or the correct forum for a particular dispute.

A careful pre-deposit check is substantially easier than reconstructing events later.

Treat documents as project tools, not mere paperwork.

Keep copies.

## Regional differences and Australian Capital Territory work

The same NSW legal framework does not automatically apply outside NSW. An Australian Capital Territory project may involve different licensing and construction-occupation administration ([ACT Government](https://www.accesscanberra.act.gov.au/business-and-work/licences-and-registrations/construction-occupations)). A contractor and homeowner should check the law and approval path for the actual site rather than carrying NSW thresholds across a border.

Within NSW, council approvals, heritage status, strata by-laws and tenancy arrangements can also change the project path; NSW planning information explains why a site-specific approval check matters ([NSW Planning](https://www.planning.nsw.gov.au/)). They do not remove the need to check the contractor and contract.
