 ## Renovation approval requirements

- Permission is required for:
  - Kitchen renovations.
  - Bathroom renovations.
  - Changes to walls, floors or ceilings.
- Permission is not required for cosmetic changes that:
  - Do not affect the structure of the property.
  - Do not require waterproofing.
- Check the strata scheme’s by-laws before starting renovation work to identify:
  - What approvals are needed.
  - How to obtain approval.
- If the correct process is not followed, the owner may have to pay to restore the property to its pre-renovation condition.

## Renovations costing over $5,000

For renovations to an apartment, duplex or other strata property costing over $5,000:

- Any strata building work must:
  - Be done safely.
  - Meet building standards.
- A registered design practitioner must prepare regulated designs.
- A registered building practitioner must oversee the work.
- The page directs readers to find a registered practitioner to assist with renovations.

## Types of renovations

### Cosmetic work

Cosmetic work does not need approval.

Cosmetic work includes changes that:

- Do not affect the structure or outside of the apartment, duplex or strata property.
- Do not require waterproofing.

Examples include:

- Installing or replacing hooks, nails, screws, handrails, blinds, curtains or built-in wardrobes.
- Painting the interior.
- Filling minor holes and cracks in internal walls.
- Laying carpet.

A strata scheme may add further types of work to its cosmetic-work list. The scheme’s by-laws should be checked.

### Minor renovations

Minor renovations need approval.

To undertake minor renovations, the owner must contact the:

- Owners corporation.
- Strata committee.
- Strata managing agent.

Permission must be requested from one of these parties.

If permission is not obtained, the owner may have to pay to restore the property to its pre-renovation condition.

Owners corporations must keep a record of each approved minor renovation for 10 years.

Minor renovations include:

- Kitchen renovations, such as installing or removing cupboards.
- Changes to internal walls.
- Changes to recessed light fittings.
- Installing or replacing wood, tile or other hard flooring, including removing carpets.
- Installing or replacing wiring, cabling, power or access points.
- Installing a rainwater tank.
- Installing a clothesline.
- Installing a reverse-cycle air conditioner.
- Installing double- or triple-glazed windows.
- Installing a heat pump.
- Installing ceiling insulation.

A strata scheme may add further types of work to its minor-renovation list. The scheme’s by-laws should be checked.

Minor renovations cannot:

- Change the outside or structure of the apartment, duplex or strata property.
- Require waterproofing.

 ## Renovating in a strata scheme

- Owners generally do not need owners corporation approval for cosmetic work such as:
  - Painting the walls inside an apartment.
  - Laying carpet.
  - Installing built-in wardrobes.
- Strata by-laws might apply to larger renovation projects.
- The page directs readers to learn more about the kinds of renovations permitted and the approval required.
- By-laws vary between strata schemes, so the by-laws for the specific scheme must be checked.

## Strata approval and by-law changes relevant to renovations

- Any owner in a strata scheme can propose or write a by-law for consideration by the owners corporation.
- The process for adding, changing or removing a strata by-law is:
  1. Write the proposal and obtain support from other owners.
  2. Write the new by-law. If there is no similar model by-law, the page states that it is best to hire a lawyer because incorrect or unclear wording can create legal issues.
  3. Write a motion and an explanatory note:
     - The motion must include the proposer’s name and an explanation of what it is for.
     - The explanatory note explains the suggested by-law and provides supporting information.
     - The explanatory note cannot be more than 300 words.
  4. Submit the by-law, motion and explanatory note to the owners corporation secretary before notice of the next general meeting is sent. The by-law and motion are then placed on the meeting agenda for a vote.
     - If they are not submitted in time, the proposer must wait for the next meeting.
  5. The owners must discuss and vote on the proposed by-law.
     - A special resolution is required.
     - No more than 25% of votes can be against the proposed by-law.
  6. The owners corporation must register the change with NSW Land Registry Services within six months of approving it.
     - Changes are not valid or enforceable until registered by NSW Land Registry Services.
     - A consolidation/change of by-laws form and a copy of the updated by-laws must be submitted.
     - The form may be completed by the strata manager or secretary but must be submitted online by someone who subscribes to an Electronic Lodgement Network Operator, usually a solicitor or conveyancer.

 ## Heritage and renovation/development

- Heritage listing does not mean that a property cannot change or adapt to meet the needs of current and future occupants and users.
- Any changes must be carefully considered and designed and undertaken in a manner that does not adversely affect the heritage significance of the place.
- Heritage information includes development on heritage properties, including properties within heritage conservation areas.
- The local heritage of the Wingecarribee Shire is managed through the **Wingecarribee Local Environmental Plan (WLEP) 2010**.
- **Clause 5.10 of the WLEP 2010** contains the planning controls for heritage places.
- **Schedule 5 of the WLEP 2010** lists:
  - Statutory heritage items
  - Archaeological sites
  - Heritage conservation areas in the Wingecarribee Shire.
- Heritage sites and areas are mapped on the **Heritage Map** forming part of the WLEP 2010.
- Heritage information includes:
  - How heritage items are assessed
  - Conservation areas
  - The State Heritage Register and Inventory
  - Benefits of owning a heritage property
  - Heritage and development.

## Checking whether a property is heritage-listed

- The NSW Planning Portal Spatial Viewer can be used to search by property address.
- The Spatial Viewer can display planning and related information, including:
  - Zoning
  - Bush fire hazard
  - Flooding
  - Heritage listings, including State Heritage Register items of State heritage significance.
- Written confirmation of heritage and other planning information can be obtained by applying for a planning certificate under **section 10.7 of the Environment Planning and Assessment Act 1979**, formerly section 149.
- A planning certificate will state, among other things, whether a property:
  - Is a heritage item; or
  - Is within a conservation area.
- Two types of certificates are available:
  - **Section 10.7(2)** — provides the statutory information.
  - **Section 10.7(2) & (5)** — provides additional information.
- The page specifically states that the **10.7(5) certificate does not provide additional heritage information**.

- The grants scheme is funded by Wingecarribee Shire Council with funding support from the NSW Government.
- Council biennially runs the Wingecarribee Heritage Awards as part of the Australian Heritage Festival, with assistance from the Wingecarribee Heritage Advisory Committee.

### Drivers of freight growth

- Population growth.
- Economic growth, resulting in increases in freight movements over and above the rate of population growth.

### Asia-related freight opportunities

- Rising populations and incomes in Asia are driving increased demand for premium agricultural products.
- This creates increased opportunities for NSW producers to export their products.
- Freight network efficiency and connectivity are critical to realising these opportunities.
- The NSW Government set a target to contribute to achieving 30 per cent growth in the value of NSW primary industries in NSW by 2020.

 ## Renovating and residential building work

- A written contract is required when the contract price is:
  - More than $5,000 including GST; or
  - Unknown, but the reasonable market cost of labour and materials is more than $5,000 including GST.
- Residential building work valued between $5,000 and $20,000 must be covered by a small jobs contract.
- Work valued at more than $20,000 must be covered by an extensive large job contract.
- The written contract must be dated and signed by, or on behalf of, each party.
- The contract may specify that work is paid for at regular intervals.
- Before entering into a contract for residential building work costing more than $5,000, builders and tradespeople must give the owner a copy of the Consumer Building Guide.
- Before signing, owners should:
  - Make sure the contract does not make them responsible for termite control instead of the builder or tradesperson.
  - Ensure progress payments are for work actually done, rather than time spent on the job.
  - Make sure the dollar value assigned to each stage of work is realistic.
  - Get information about insurance.
  - Be clear about the duration of warranties.
  - Discuss anything they do not understand with the builder or tradesperson.
  - Not sign if they are unhappy, because they have the right to request changes to the contract.
  - Obtain legal advice before changing a standard contract, or if the builder or tradesperson has amended a standard contract or included special conditions.
- Arbitration clauses are not permitted in a home building contract and are deemed void.
- A licence in the name of an individual does not permit that individual’s company or partnership to make the contract, even if the individual is a company director or a member of the partnership.
- If a company or partnership is making the contract, it must be licensed in the company or partnership name.
- The page advises checking the contractor’s details using the home building online licence check before signing.

 ## NSW construction-contract variations

- Updated: 1 Jul 2008.
- Guidance applies to contracts for construction work and agreements for related services.
- Terminology:
  - “Contract” includes construction contracts and service agreements.
  - “Contractor” includes contractors, consultants and project managers.
  - “Principal’s representative” means the principal’s authorised person or the principal’s representative, as applicable.
- In general terms, a variation is a change to the work required under the contract.
- For a construction contract, a variation is usually defined as a change to the works. Each standard-form contract contains additional provisions relating to variations and their management.
- If the principal’s representative directs a variation, the contractor is obliged to carry out the instruction.
- The contractor is generally not entitled to change the works without agreement confirmed in writing by the principal’s representative.
- A variation should generally be consistent with, or similar in nature to, the work under the contract.
- A variation becomes part of the contract. All contract conditions apply to the changed work, including provisions for:
  - extensions of time
  - site conditions, if applicable.
- A variation instruction will often lead to contractor claims for:
  - additional payment
  - an extension to the period for completion.
- Additional payment may include costs associated with:
  - delay
  - acceleration
  - disruption.
- Variations are identified as one of the main reasons for cost and time overruns.
- The approved scope of work should not be changed without good reason.
- It is not the role of the principal’s representative to improve the approved design.

## Categories of variations

### Unavoidable variations

- Unavoidable variations are necessary to minimise adverse effects caused by unexpected events or circumstances.
- They may be required to avoid:
  - health problems
  - safety problems
  - security problems.
- They do not result in a change to the scope of the work.
- Examples include:
  - minimising increased cost or another adverse impact caused by a latent condition, such as:
    - unanticipated ground conditions
    - hazardous materials
    - existing services
  - overcoming a fault in the principal’s design or documentation, such as:
    - an error
    - ambiguity
    - inconsistency
  - overcoming such a fault where it is not an omission or lack of completeness that may be the contractor’s responsibility, and where failing to remedy it could:
    - result in health, safety or security problems
    - prevent work from continuing
  - overcoming a change in statutory requirements that occurred since tenders closed.
- Failure to give an instruction in these circumstances may:
  - prevent completion of the contract
  - constitute a breach of contract by the principal.
